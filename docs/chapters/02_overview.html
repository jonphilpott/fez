FEZ is two machines joined by a set of numbers. One makes sound. The other decides, every five milliseconds, what the sound should do next. The numbers they share are called registers.

The sound path

Three sources feed the mixer: a complex oscillator (a sine blended with a second wave, then bent by the wavefolder), a mod oscillator and noise. The mixer's sum goes through the lowpass gate. The gate does two jobs with one control: it sets how loud a note is and how bright. A note that fades also dulls, which is why FEZ sounds struck rather than switched.

The mod voice is a second path, built for slow sweeps. It takes the same mod oscillator and the same noise as the mixer does, and runs them through a ladder filter of its own. The buffer device records one of four things, as the register BWSRC chooses: the mixer's sum, the voice, the mod voice, or both voices together. It plays the recording back either into the mixer or straight on to the effects.

The three paths are then added together, and from here on everything is treated alike. First comes the tape delay, then the spring, then the compressor. Until the spring the signal is a single channel. The spring's two tanks give each ear its own reverb, so the sound turns stereo there and stays so. The compressor has no controls. It holds the level down when the loops build up, so that a delay at full feedback cannot overload the output.

The control path

Nothing in FEZ happens by itself except time. Four pulsers keep it, firing as often as you set, in pulses a minute. When a pulser fires, each block that listens to it runs once.

A block is a short program in FEZ's own language. It can read any register and write most of them: change a pitch, open a filter, fire another pulser, leave a number for another block to find. Between runs a block remembers only what is in the registers (and, after YIELD, where it stopped), which is why the registers sit at the centre of the picture.

Devices add numbers that nobody typed. The cellular automaton, the Lorenz attractor, the sequencer, the Turing machine and the keyboard each write their results into registers. Some fire pulsers (the automaton, the attractor, the keyboard). Others are stepped by them (the sequencer, the Turing machine, the grains of the buffer). A pattern in one device can therefore become notes in another.

The matrix lies between the registers and the sound. Each route takes a source, multiplies it by a weight and adds the result to a register's value. The sound reads that sum, not the number you wrote.

A register holds what you wrote. What you hear is that value plus whatever the matrix adds. If a knob seems to have no effect, check the matrix: a route may be adding an opposite amount.
