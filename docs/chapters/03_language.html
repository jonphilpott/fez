The language is small: 46 words, a stack and some registers. It is a cousin of Forth, an old language built on a stack. You do not need to know Forth. Everything here is explained from nothing, and the whole language fits on one page of the word reference at the end of the manual.

The stack

Think of a pile of cards. A number you write goes on top of the pile. A word that needs numbers takes them from the top, and puts its answer back on the pile. That pile is the stack, and it replaces the brackets and variables you may know from other languages.

2 3 +

Read left to right. 2 goes on the stack, then 3 goes on top of it, and + takes both and leaves 5. There are no brackets because the order does the work: to compute (2 + 3) minus 1, you write 2 3 + 1 -.

The stack holds 16 numbers. Taking from an empty stack, or putting a seventeenth on a full one, is a fault (see below).

Stack pictures

Every word in the reference has a picture of what it does to the stack, such as ( a b -- c ). Everything left of the two dashes is what the word takes, and everything on the right is what it leaves. The top of the stack is on the right. So - is ( a b -- a-b ): it takes b from the top, a from beneath it, and leaves a minus b. 10 3 - leaves 7.

Four words move numbers about without changing them. DUP copies the top, DROP throws it away, SWAP trades the top two, and OVER copies the second to the top. ROT brings the third to the top: after 1 2 3 ROT the stack holds 2, 3, 1 (top).

Numbers

A number can be written four ways, and each is one token:

12000       \ a whole number, 0 to 32767
0x2A54      \ the same sort of number in hexadecimal (base 16)
.5          \ a fraction of full scale: .5 is 16384, half of 32768
A4          \ a note name: C-1 to G9; # sharp, b flat

A fraction is a number between 0 and 1 written with a point. It is stored as that fraction of 32768, so .25 is 8192 and .99 is 32440. Fractions are the natural way to write a register that runs from nothing to full, such as a filter cutoff. A note name is stored as a pitch; see Pitch for what that number means. A note name always includes its octave: C4 is middle C.

Tokens and slots

A program is a row of tokens separated by spaces: a number, a note name, a register name or a word. Each token takes exactly one slot, which is one place in the block's memory. A block has 128 slots and a word has 24, and the editor shows how many you have used. There is no trick in the counting: five tokens are five slots.

That has a consequence for numbers. A number has no sign. To write -5 you write 5 NEG: the number, then the word that changes its sign. That is two tokens and two slots, and you can see both. If you type -5 the editor says no negative numbers: write 5 NEG and refuses to run the block.

5 NEG        \ -5
.5 NEG       \ minus a half
12 ST NEG    \ twelve semitones down: an octave below

The rule is kept this strict on purpose, so that SKIP, which jumps over the next slot, always jumps over exactly the next token you can see.

Numbers stop at the ends

Every number in FEZ is a whole number from -32768 to 32767, the range that fits in 16 binary digits (the "16 bit" you will see in the source). Most programming languages let a number that runs past one end wrap round to the other: add 1 to 32767 and you get -32768. FEZ does not. A result that goes past either end sticks there. The usual word for this is saturating: the number is full and takes no more.

30000 5000 +        \ 32767, not 35000
30000 NEG 5000 -    \ -32768, not -35000
32767 NEG 1 -       \ -32768
32767 NEG 1 - NEG   \ 32767: -32768 has no positive twin

Sticking is the right behaviour for sound. A cutoff pushed past fully open should stay open. If it wrapped, it would jump to fully shut, and you would hear a crack.

The check happens at every step, not at the end, so the order of a sum matters. 30000 5000 + 5000 - leaves 27767, because the first sum was cut to 32767 before 5000 was taken off. Take away first and add after, 30000 5000 - 5000 +, and you get 30000. The word */, which multiplies and then divides, keeps the full product between its two steps, so 20000 20000 20000 */ correctly leaves 20000.

The words that stop at the ends are +, -, *, /, */, NEG, ABS and SHL. The bit words (AND, OR, XOR, NOT, SHR, ROL and ROR) work on the 16 binary digits as they are and never stick: 1 NEG 4 SHR leaves 4095, because the digits move right and zeros come in at the top. Words that only compare or choose (MIN, MAX, =, <, >) cannot overflow.

Registers stop at their own ends too. Most hold 0 to 32767, so 5 NEG CUTOFF ! leaves the cutoff at 0 and 30000 5000 + CUTOFF ! leaves it at 32767. The registers meant for signed numbers (CV0 to CV3 and the scratch slots S0 to S15) hold -32768 to 32767.

A number you type is limited as well. The largest is 32767, and a larger one is an error when you apply the block (literal out of range), not a silent cut. The most negative number, -32768, cannot be typed at all: write 32767 NEG 1 -.

Two kinds of multiply

* multiplies whole numbers, as + and - add and subtract them: 6 7 * leaves 42. Sound, however, is mostly proportions: half as loud, a quarter as bright. For that there is F*, the fraction multiply, in which 32768 stands for 1.0. So .5 .5 F* leaves .25 (8192), and 20000 .5 F* leaves 10000: half of 20000. Use F* to scale a register by a proportion, and * for counting. Mixing them up is easy to spot: 6 7 F* leaves 0, because six thirty-two-thousandths of seven thirty-two-thousandths is too small to count.

To multiply and then divide by a whole number, use */: 100 3 4 */ leaves 75. It keeps the full product between its two steps, so a big intermediate number does not stick.

Registers

A register is a named number that something else reads. The voice reads CUTOFF and PITCH; the delay reads DLYTIME. Each has an address, a small whole number, and the name is only a nickname for it. So when you write CUTOFF, what goes on the stack is the address.

  • @ reads: ( address -- value ). CUTOFF @ leaves the cutoff on the stack.
  • ! writes: ( value address -- ). 12000 CUTOFF ! stores 12000 there.

The value goes first because it must already be on the stack when the address arrives. Registers keep their own limits. Most hold 0 to 32767, and a few are read-only, such as KBGATE; writing to one is a fault.

To add one to a register, read it, add, and write it back:

CUTOFF @ 100 + CUTOFF !

Scratch registers and private ones

You will often want somewhere to keep a number between ticks. There are two places. S0 to S15 are sixteen scratch registers that every block can see, so blocks can leave each other messages. P0 to P15 are private: each block has its own, and no other block can touch them. Use P for a counter that belongs to one block, and S to share. Both hold -32768 to 32767.

P0 @ 1 + P0 !     \ count how many times this block has run

Blocks, words and ticks

Code lives in blocks. There are sixteen, each up to 128 slots long. A block does nothing until something runs it: a pulser, the sequencer, a key press, or another block. When it runs it goes once from top to bottom and stops. It does not loop on its own.

A word is a short program kept apart from the blocks, 24 slots at most, that any block can run with W. 3 W runs word 3. Words share the stack with whoever calls them, so a word can take its inputs and leave its answer there, like a built-in. There are sixteen.

FEZ works in steps called ticks. One tick is five milliseconds, so there are 200 a second. In each tick the pulsers that are due fire, the blocks they started run, and the registers settle on their new values for the sound to read. A block can therefore change anything it likes, as often as it likes, and what you hear is the state at the end of each tick. Blocks run between stretches of sound, never during one, so a block cannot make the sound glitch by running late.

A block also has a safety limit of 256 steps per run. A loop that never ends stops there and is reported as a fault, instead of freezing the instrument.

When something goes wrong

A fault is a mistake caught while a block runs. Some stop the block: taking from an empty stack, a jump to a label that is not there, a loop that reaches the step limit. The block halts, and the editor shows HALTED in red with one line on what went wrong, such as ! needs 2, stack had 1, and marks the word that caused it with a red block. If the fault happened inside a word you defined, the line starts in a word: and the mark is on the W that called it. Others let the block run on, because a sensible answer exists: dividing by zero gives 0, and writing to a register that cannot be written does nothing. These show the same kind of line in amber, such as ! to KBGATE: cannot be written. The rest of FEZ carries on either way. Press Ctrl+Enter to apply the block again, which clears the fault and restarts it.

Division by zero is the one gentle fault. 1 0 / leaves 0, notes the fault, and the block carries on.

Making decisions and loops

A label is a name for a place in a block. 3 L marks the place as label 3 (there are 16, numbered 0 to 15). 3 JMP jumps to it. JZ jumps if a number is zero, and JNZ jumps if it is not:

0 S0 !                 \ start the count at 0
1 L                    \ label 1: the top of the loop
S0 @ 1 + S0 !          \ add one
S0 @ 4 < 1 JNZ         \ if S0 is below 4, go back to label 1

The comparison 4 < leaves 1 for true or 0 for false, which is the flag that JNZ tests. After this runs, S0 holds 4.

For the commonest small decision there is ?, which picks one of two numbers: ( a b flag -- a or b ) leaves b if the flag is not zero and a if it is. And SKIP takes a flag and, if it is 0, skips the very next token.

Two words stop a block early or in pieces. EXIT stops it for now. YIELD stops it and, the next time the block runs, carries on from that point, not from the top. That makes a step-by-step block simple to write:

S0 @ 1 + S0 !          \ first run: add one, then pause
YIELD
S0 @ 10 + S0 !         \ second run: add ten, then start again

Rotating a pattern

A number is also a row of 16 on-or-off bits, and a row of bits is a pattern: which steps of a rhythm sound, which cells of the automaton are alive. ROL and ROR slide such a pattern round like a ring. The bits that fall off one end come back in at the other, so none are lost. SHL and SHR cannot do this: SHL is a multiplication that sticks at the top (see Numbers stop at the ends), and both fill with zeros.

Here is a complete rhythm. The pattern is 16 steps, one bit each: a 1 plays a note and a 0 is a rest. Run this once to set it:

0x1249 S0 !          \ the pattern: a note every third step

Then give a block to a pulser. Each time it runs it looks at the bottom bit, plays if it is set, and turns the pattern one place so the next bit comes round:

S0 @ 1 AND 1 JZ      \ bottom bit 0? stay silent
1 GATE !             \ bottom bit 1: play a note
1 L
S0 @ 1 ROR S0 !      \ turn the pattern one place right

After 16 runs the pattern is back where it began. Change a bit of S0 while it plays and the rhythm changes at once. ROL turns the pattern the other way. The same trick works on the automaton's row: CAROW @ 3 ROR CAROW ! turns it three cells round.

Pitch

In FEZ a pitch is an ordinary whole number. One semitone, the step between two neighbouring piano keys, is 256. An octave is twelve semitones, so it is 3072.

The rule for turning a note into a number is to take its MIDI note number and multiply by 256. Middle C is MIDI note 60, so it is 60 × 256 = 15360. The A above it, the note orchestras tune to, is note 69, so it is 17664. The register PITCH holds this number.

Numbers that fall between the whole semitones are the notes between the keys. 128 more than a note is a quarter-tone sharp of it. One unit is about four-tenths of a cent, and a cent is a hundredth of a semitone, finer than anyone can hear.

Programmers call this 8.8 fixed point, and you will see the phrase in the source code. It means that the 16-bit number is split in two: the top eight bits count whole semitones and the bottom eight count 256ths of one. You never need to think in bits. "Multiply by 256" is the whole rule. The reason for doing it this way is that whole numbers give the same answer on every computer, including the small one inside the TrimUI Brick, with no rounding surprises. The same trick, with a different split, is behind the fractions such as .5.

There are three ways to write a pitch. They give the same kind of number, so you can mix them:

C4          \ a note name: 15360
60 ST       \ 60 semitones: 15360 again
4400 HZ     \ 440 Hz in tenths of a hertz: 17664, the A

To transpose, add. ST turns a number of semitones into the right amount to add, so a fifth up is seven semitones:

PITCH @ 7 ST + PITCH !      \ add a fifth to the pitch

To go down, make the amount negative: 12 ST NEG is an octave below. Registers hold 0 to 32767, so the top of the range is note 127 (G9).

The word COLOUR works on the same scale: it gives the distance above a root note, and you add the root yourself.
